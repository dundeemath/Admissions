Crossing the current: vectors in the water

Vectors
Mechanics
Trigonometry

Imagine you’re standing on the bank of a wide river, and you want to swim to the point directly opposite – straight across, no detour. The obvious plan is to just swim straight ahead, aimed at that exact point. It doesn’t work. If the river has any current at all, aiming straight across sweeps you downstream, and you land well short of where you meant to.

This is one of the cleanest real demonstrations of vector addition there is, because the two velocities involved are so easy to picture: your own swimming velocity (a speed and a direction, entirely under your control) and the river’s current (a speed and direction you can’t change, fixed by the water). What actually happens – the path you trace across the river – is neither of those on its own. It’s their sum:

\[\vec{R} = \vec{u}_{\text{swim}} + \vec{v}_{\text{current}}\]

Your resultant velocity \(\vec{R}\) is what a bystander on the bank would actually see: two velocities, added tip-to-tail, giving a single path over the ground.

The fix is counterintuitive: aim upstream. If you angle your swimming effort partly against the current, that upstream component can exactly cancel the current’s sideways push, leaving a resultant that points straight across after all – even though you, personally, are never aimed straight at the target. The app below draws exactly this vector triangle live: your swim vector, the current vector tacked onto its tip, and the resultant they add up to.

The exact angle has a clean formula. If your swim speed (relative to the water) is \(u\) and the current’s speed is \(v_c\), the heading \(\theta\) (measured from straight across, aimed upstream) that cancels the current exactly satisfies

\[\sin\theta = \frac{v_c}{u} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{v_c}{u}\right)\]

Try matching this in the app: the dashed line marks where “directly opposite” is, and ticking the box overlays exactly the heading this formula predicts. Turn your own heading slider until your solid trajectory line lands right on top of it.

But notice this only has a solution when \(v_c \le u\). If the current is faster than you can swim, no heading – however cleverly chosen – ever produces zero drift. You can reduce the drift by aiming further upstream, but you can never cancel it, because your maximum possible sideways (upstream) contribution is capped at your own swim speed \(u\), and no angle lets you exceed it. This isn’t just a maths curiosity – it’s exactly why lifeguards and open-water safety training teach never to swim directly against a strong current or rip: the current wins that contest outright. The safer response, in the real case a straight crossing is impossible, is to swim at an angle that at least minimises drift while still making progress across – itself a nice extension of this problem for anyone who wants to differentiate the drift formula and find where it’s minimised, rather than merely zero.
