Taking off: the mathematics of lift

Calculus
Differential equations
Physics

Watch a plane take off and there’s a strange pause built into the whole event: it doesn’t simply lift off the tarmac. It accelerates down a runway for the best part of a minute, engines at full power, going nowhere but forward – and only once it’s built up real speed does it leave the ground at all. Why does it need that runway? Why can’t a fully-powered aircraft just lift straight up from a standing start?

The answer is sitting inside one equation. The lift a wing generates is

\[L = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\, v^2\, A\, C_L(\alpha)\]

where \(\rho\) is the air density, \(v\) is the aircraft’s airspeed, \(A\) is the wing area, and \(C_L(\alpha)\) – the lift coefficient – depends on the wing’s angle of attack \(\alpha\): how sharply it’s tilted into the oncoming air. Everything a pilot can actually control on takeoff is hiding in just two of those symbols, \(v\) and \(\alpha\), and the app below lets you play with exactly those two.

Why speed first. Notice that \(v\) appears squared. Double the airspeed and you don’t double the lift – you quadruple it. At a standstill, \(v=0\), and no angle of attack in the world produces any lift at all: the “lift versus speed” curve in the app starts flat on the ground and only turns upward once real airspeed builds. A runway isn’t bureaucracy – it’s the aircraft physically climbing that curve until lift finally catches up to weight.

Why angle matters, up to a point. Tilt the wing more steeply into the airflow and \(C_L\) rises too – for small angles, very close to linearly, which is why even a small stick movement noticeably changes how a plane climbs. But push the angle too far and the airflow over the top of the wing can no longer follow the surface: it separates, lift collapses even as the angle keeps increasing, and the wing stalls. The app models this honestly – push the angle-of-attack slider far enough and you’ll watch the lift curve break and fall, exactly as a real wing’s does.

Why the wing’s shape itself changes on takeoff. If you’ve ever watched a plane depart from a window seat, you’ll have seen panels unfold from the trailing edge of the wing just before liftoff – flaps, sometimes slats at the leading edge too. Deploying them effectively adds camber (curvature) to the wing, and that shifts the whole lift curve upward: more lift at any given speed, which means a lower speed is enough to fly. It’s a direct trade against efficiency – flown deployed at cruising speed, flaps create far more drag than they’re worth – so they come out only for the low-speed phases, takeoff and landing, where the extra lift is worth the cost. Tick the flaps checkbox in the app and watch both the wing’s shape and the required takeoff speed change together.

A note on what’s really happening in the airflow. It’s tempting to reach for the popular explanation that air has to travel faster over the curved top of a wing to “meet up” with the air going under it at the trailing edge, and that this alone explains the lower pressure and hence the lift (via Bernoulli’s principle). That specific story – equal transit time – isn’t actually correct, and real aerodynamics doesn’t need it. What actually happens is that the wing deflects the oncoming air downward as it passes; by Newton’s third law, the air pushes back on the wing with an equal and opposite force – upward lift. The precise, rigorous version of this is written in the Euler equations for an inviscid fluid, and solving them for flow around a wing shape shows that the airfoil sets up a circulation of air around itself which, combined with the free-stream flow, produces exactly the pressure difference (faster, lower-pressure flow over the top; slower, higher-pressure flow underneath) that Bernoulli’s principle then correctly relates to lift. Bernoulli isn’t wrong here – the popular shortcut for why the speeds differ in the first place is.

The model used in the app below is a further simplification of all this – thin-airfoil theory, which predicts \(C_L(\alpha) \approx 2\pi\sin\alpha\) for small angles – plus a hand-built stall curve, rather than genuine wind-tunnel data. It’s accurate enough to demonstrate both qualitative facts this page is built around – that lift needs speed and angle together, and that too much angle defeats itself – without pretending to be a real aircraft’s flight-test numbers.
