Long before calculus existed

Section title card only - say nothing yet, let it sit for a second, then start the first story. No mention of “the wave equation” by name anywhere in this section.

A blacksmith’s forge

Pythagoras, according to legend, noticed that hammers of different weights striking an anvil rang out notes that sounded good together — but only in certain simple ratios.

First hint in recorded history that music has numbers hiding inside it. Keep it as a told story, not a slide to read from.

1636

Marin Mersenne, in Paris, carefully measuring strings by hand — and writing down the first correct laws linking a string’s length, tension and thickness to its pitch.

He got it right over a century before calculus existed to prove why.

A very heated argument

Three of the 18th century’s greatest mathematicians — d’Alembert, Euler, and Daniel Bernoulli — fell out for years over what a vibrating string’s motion actually is.

That argument is where our equation was born.

Joseph Fourier

Any sound at all — however messy, however many instruments playing at once — is secretly just a stack of pure, simple notes.

That single idea is why we’re about to take a real sound apart and see what’s inside it.

The guitar has stayed untouched on the table through this whole section. You’ve planted it; you haven’t returned to it yet.

This is mathematics.

THE TURN. Name it out loud: “the wave equation” - one of the oldest and most-used equations there is. Don’t write the equation itself on screen here, and don’t derive it - the next slide gives the one number it produces, which is the level this audience needs.

\[ f=\dfrac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}} \]

Pluck a string of length \(L\), under tension \(T\), with mass-per-length \(\mu\) — and it must ring out at this frequency.

This is the only equation on screen in the whole talk. Say the plain- English version immediately after (next slide) - don’t linger on the symbols themselves.

HARMONIC SLIDER - this is the same n that appears in the frequency formula and in the appendix derivation (u(x,t)=sin(npix/L)cos(…), kL=n*pi). Bumping it from 1 to 2 or 3 redraws the standing-wave shape with extra humps/nodes and multiplies the predicted frequency by n - this is the live, hands-on version of the “harmonics are the only shapes the string is allowed to vibrate in” slide later in the talk (the one right after “your turn”). Not part of the core 9-minute demo script, but worth a quick “watch what happens if I go to the second harmonic” aside if time allows, or as a callback when you reach that later slide. The predicted-spectrogram axis auto-extends past 1000 Hz if a high harmonic on a light string pushes the frequency above it, so the line never runs off the top of the plot.

SLIDER RANGES - match the “Real strings, real numbers” table two slides back (D’Addario EJ23 9-45 Super Light, DADGAD). Default values land on the low D string: T=65N, mu=7.18 g/m -> ~73 Hz at n=1. High D is reachable at T=46N, mu=0.32 g/m -> ~292 Hz (two octaves up, as promised). These come from D’Addario’s own published tension chart for that gauge, not a rough estimate - measure/tune-check the real guitar beforehand anyway and nudge the sliders’ default values in the .qmd if your set differs. Nice point to make live: L barely needs to change between the two strings (same scale length) - it’s T and mu that do the work, which is why two strings the same length ring out notes two octaves apart.

Why this is a maths degree, not a party trick

Zoom out. Keep every point here to one line - these are meant to be recognised, not taught. No equations in this section at all.

This equation has cousins.

The same shape of equation describes heat flowing through metal, the wavefunctions of quantum mechanics, and why bridges — and eardrums — vibrate the way they do.

Fourier is running on your phone, right now.

The same idea behind that spectrogram is how Shazam recognises a song in ten seconds, how MP3s compress music, and how an MRI scanner builds a picture from radio waves.

Those harmonics — \(n=1,2,3\dots\) — are the only shapes the string is allowed to vibrate in.

Almost the same idea reappears, barely changed, in quantum mechanics.

Change the ends, change the notes.

It’s because the string is pinned at both ends that only whole-number harmonics are allowed at all. Change the boundary, and you change the whole allowed spectrum.

This kind of thinking is what a maths degree trains — and it’s valued.

Appendix: where the formula comes from

1. Newton’s second law on a small piece of string (tension \(T\), mass per length \(\mu\)) gives the wave equation — curvature \(\propto\) acceleration: \[\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2}=c^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}, \qquad c=\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}.\]

2. Any sinusoid \(u(x,t)=\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)\) solves this, for any \(k\), provided \(\omega = ck\) — that’s just what the wave equation means.

3. Pinned at both ends: \(u(0,t)=0\) is automatic; \(u(L,t)=0\) forces \(\sin(kL)=0\), so \(kL=n\pi\), \(n=1,2,3,\dots\) — only certain wavelengths fit.

4. Combine \(k=n\pi/L\) with \(\omega=ck\): since \(f=\omega/2\pi\), \[f=\frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}.\]

BONUS SLIDE - not part of the 30-minute talk, and not shown unless someone asks “where does that formula actually come from?” (an Advanced Higher pupil or a teacher, most likely, in Q&A). Step 1 (deriving c = sqrt(T/mu) from F=ma on a string element) is its own multi-line derivation involving small-angle approximations - say that it exists rather than deriving it live unless someone asks for that specifically too. Steps 2-4 are the part most Higher/Advanced Higher pupils can actually follow, since they only need trig identities and the idea of a partial derivative, not the force-balance argument.
