Demo activities — Greenfields Academy, Wednesday

Companion to GreenfieldsAcademy_NoteIsTheEquation.qmd. These are the hands-on moments that go around the talk — what to actually do with your hands and the room, not just what’s on screen.

Materials checklist

  • Guitar or the DIY monochord, already tuned before pupils arrive
  • Tape measure
  • A way to read tension: either a tuner (read the note, back-calculate) or a small hanging weight of known mass on a pulley/lever rig
  • Laptop with the deck open, mic permission already granted in the browser (test this in the actual room — see below)
  • Projector with working sound, for the cold-open plucks to be heard clearly at the back
  • A phone or second laptop open to a tone-generator site (e.g. szynalski.com/tone-generator) as a source of a known, exact frequency — useful for the “name that sound” activity below
  • Chalkboard/whiteboard for writing the three measured numbers (L, T, μ) live

Before Wednesday

  1. Test the live-recording slide in the actual room, not just at your desk. Classroom acoustics and background hum/chatter are the single biggest risk to this talk — much bigger than the maths. If the room is noisy, plan to do the recording activity during a quieter moment (e.g. right after asking for silence) rather than mid-chatter.
  2. Have a pre-recorded spectrogram screenshot of a clean guitar pluck ready as a fallback slide/image, in case the live mic doesn’t read clearly on the day.
  3. Measure the guitar/monochord’s real L, T and μ once beforehand so you know roughly what predicted frequency to expect — you’ll still do the measuring live, but you won’t be caught out by a wildly wrong number.
  4. Check the school’s wifi/browser policy allows microphone access — shinylive apps run entirely in the browser (no server round-trip needed once loaded), so once the page is loaded it should keep working even if wifi drops, but the initial load needs a working connection.

The activities, in order

1. Cold open — the two-pluck question (0:00–2:00)

Pluck the low string, let it ring fully. Pluck the high string, let it ring fully. Ask the room: “how many times higher is that second note?” Take one or two guesses, don’t confirm or deny — just move into the stories.

2. Predict & measure — the centrepiece (9:00–14:00)

This is the core demo:

  • Measure L, T and μ on the real instrument, live, writing the numbers on the board.
  • Set those same three numbers on the sliders in the “Predict the Note” app. Read the predicted frequency out loud.
  • Pluck the real string, switch to the “Record & See” app, press record, and compare where the real peak lands against the predicted number.
  • Let the comparison land in silence for a second before commenting on it — don’t rush to explain the gap (or the pleasing lack of one) away.

3. Change one variable — volunteer activity (14:00–18:00)

Bring a volunteer up. Have them either: - move a finger/capo partway along the string (changes L), or - turn a tuning peg a little tighter or looser (changes T)

Ask them to predict out loud, before plucking, whether the note will go up or down. Let the room check the prediction against their own ears, then check it against the “Record & See” spectrogram.

4. Optional bonus — “name that sound” (if time allows, or during Q&A)

A quick guessing game using the recording app. For each sound, ask the room to guess what the spectrogram will look like before you reveal it — one thin line, several thin lines, or a broad smear:

Sound What it should show
A pure tone from the tone-generator site at a known Hz (e.g. 440 Hz) One thin, exact horizontal line at that frequency
Someone hissing “shhh” (white noise) A broad smear across all frequencies, no clean line
Singing a short scale (do–re–mi…) A line that steps up in clean jumps
Three pupils humming different notes of a chord together Several thin parallel lines at once

This is a nice bridge into the “widen the lens” section — it’s the same Fourier idea Shazam and MP3s use, just made visible.

5. Optional bonus — whose voice is whose? (if time allows)

Record two pupils saying the same vowel sound (“aaah”) one after another. Compare the two spectrograms — same rough pitch, but the fine pattern of peaks (formants) differs person to person. Nice, personal way to show that a spectrogram carries more information than just “the note.”

Timing cheat sheet (30 minutes)

Time What’s happening
0:00–2:00 Cold open — two plucks, no explanation
2:00–7:00 Stories (Pythagoras, Mersenne, the d’Alembert/Euler/Bernoulli argument, Fourier)
7:00–9:00 Name it — the formula reveal
9:00–18:00 Centrepiece: measure → predict → record → volunteer changes a variable
18:00–25:00 Widen the lens (equation’s cousins, Fourier on your phone, harmonics, boundary conditions, your own “why I find this compelling”)
25:00–28:00 Close — final pluck, closing line
28:00–30:00 Q&A — good moment for the “name that sound” bonus activity if it didn’t fit earlier

If the live mic fails on the day

Switch straight to the pre-recorded spectrogram screenshot (see prep step 2), say plainly that you’re showing a recording made earlier rather than live, and carry on — the predict/measure part of the demo (steps 1–2 above) still lands the core point even without a working live mic.
